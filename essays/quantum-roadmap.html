What a qubit can’t do for your roadmap

Quantum computers are real and mostly misunderstood. A product builder’s guide to what they change, what they don’t, and the one thing to start on now.

An explainer for people who build products, not physicists. Estimates and dates are as published; the field moves quickly.

01 /

Not a faster computer

The most common misunderstanding about quantum computers is that they are very fast ordinary computers. They are not. For most tasks, from serving a web page to training a recommendation model, they offer no advantage at all, and in practice they are slower and far more expensive.

What they offer is a different kind of computation. The state of n qubits is described by 2ⁿ complex numbers called amplitudes. Fifty qubits already need about a quadrillion of them. But you cannot read those numbers out: a measurement returns just n ordinary bits, chosen at random with probabilities set by the amplitudes. The power is not in storing many answers. It is in interference: arranging the computation so the amplitudes of wrong answers cancel each other out and the amplitudes of right answers add up.

That only works when a problem has a structure the algorithm can exploit. No structure, no speedup. This one sentence removes most of the hype.

02 /

Interference, step by step

Grover’s search algorithm is the clearest example. Imagine eight possible answers, one of them correct, and a test that can recognise the right one. Classically you check them one by one: on average you need several tries. Grover starts with every answer equally likely, then repeats one step that flips the sign of the correct answer’s amplitude and reflects all amplitudes around their average. Each repetition moves probability toward the correct answer.

Notice what the speedup is: quadratic, not magical. And the “test that recognises the right answer” must itself run on the quantum computer. For a real database, a classical index already finds a record in a handful of steps, so Grover helps nothing there. Its value is in search problems where no shortcut exists.

03 /

Where the speedups are

A small number of problem families have strong quantum algorithms. Most of what product teams do is not in them.

Chemistry and materials are the most credible near-term wins, because nature itself is quantum and classical computers struggle to simulate it exactly. Factoring matters for a different reason: it is the problem the internet’s security quietly depends on.

04 /

Noise is the whole story

Today’s machines have hundreds to around a thousand physical qubits, and every one of them is noisy. Operations fail at small but significant rates, and errors accumulate faster than a long algorithm can finish. John Preskill named this era NISQ: noisy intermediate-scale quantum.

The way out is quantum error correction: encoding one reliable logical qubit across many physical ones, and continuously detecting and correcting errors without destroying the computation. The overhead is large, often hundreds or thousands of physical qubits per logical qubit. The crucial milestone is being below threshold, where adding more physical qubits makes the logical qubit better rather than worse. In late 2024 Google reported exactly that with its Willow chip. It does not make useful quantum computers arrive tomorrow, but it moved the question from whether to when.

05 /

The deadline that is already here

Shor’s algorithm, on a large error-corrected machine, would break RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography: the public-key systems behind HTTPS, VPNs, software updates and digital signatures. Published estimates for breaking RSA-2048 have fallen from about 20 million noisy qubits (Gidney and Ekerå, 2019) to under a million (Gidney, 2025). Nobody can build that yet. But attackers do not need to wait: traffic recorded today can be stored and decrypted later. This is called harvest now, decrypt later.

The replacement exists. In August 2024 NIST published the first post-quantum cryptography standards: FIPS 203 (ML-KEM) for key exchange, and FIPS 204 (ML-DSA) and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA) for signatures. Chrome and Cloudflare already use hybrid key exchange that combines a classical algorithm with ML-KEM, so much of today’s web traffic is protected without anyone noticing.

06 /

What to do on Monday

For a product team, the quantum to-do list is short and has nothing to do with qubits. First, inventory where your product uses public-key cryptography: TLS, tokens, signed URLs, stored encrypted data, third-party SDKs. Second, build crypto agility: keep algorithms behind interfaces and configuration, so changing them is a deployment, not a rewrite. Third, ask your vendors and cloud providers for their post-quantum plans. If you want to experiment, the Open Quantum Safe project ships liboqs with Python bindings.

And keep the hype off the roadmap. A pitch that promises quantum speedups for your recommendation engine or logistics in the next few years deserves the same question as any other: what is the structure being exploited, and has a classical method already matched it? The real quantum work for most teams is defensive, unglamorous and already overdue.

Take this with you

Quantum changes a few things completely, and most things not at all.

  • Speedups need structure: chemistry, factoring and some search, not general computing.
  • Error correction below threshold is the milestone to watch, not raw qubit counts.
  • Start the post-quantum cryptography migration now; it is a software project you can do today.
