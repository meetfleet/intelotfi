One appearance is not a perfect record

How a Wilson lower bound keeps thin show-up evidence from becoming an overconfident trust signal.

01 /

A rate without its denominator

Someone appears at the one event for which you have a recorded outcome. Their observed show-up rate is 100%. Another person appears at ten out of ten. Their observed rate is also 100%. The fractions are equal; the amount of evidence is not. A display that treats both as equally established hides the most important difference.

The problem is not that the first person has done anything wrong. It is that one observation cannot support the same confidence as a longer, comparable record. “Perfect so far” describes the data. “Perfectly reliable” makes a much larger claim about the future.

A Wilson lower confidence bound offers one way to keep that gap visible. Rather than rewarding a thin record with a maximum signal, it combines the observed proportion with sample size. The result is a cautious statistical summary of a defined behavior, not a moral measure of the person behind it.

02 /

A lower bound, not a verdict

Start with a Bernoulli outcome: a recorded eligible event is classified as a show-up or a non-show-up. Let s be the show-ups and n the total recorded outcomes. The observed proportion is p̂ = s/n. Under the binomial model, the Wilson interval estimates a range for the underlying show-up probability while accounting for sampling uncertainty.

Here the chosen signal is the lower endpoint of the 95% two-sided Wilson interval, using z = 1.96. This is not the same convention as a 95% one-sided lower bound. Naming the convention matters because a different confidence level changes the number. For no recorded outcomes, n = 0, this formula is undefined; the honest state is “not enough evidence,” not a fabricated zero.

03 /

One success versus ten

When all observed outcomes are successes, p̂ = 1 and the lower endpoint simplifies to n/(n + z²). With z = 1.96, z² = 3.8416. For one show-up in one recorded event, the lower endpoint is 1/4.8416 ≈ 0.207. For ten out of ten, it is 10/13.8416 ≈ 0.722. For fifty out of fifty, it is approximately 0.929.

Nothing about the observed rate changed: it is still 100% in every example. What changes is how much the evidence narrows the interval. A low lower endpoint after one observation does not mean that the person is predicted to appear only 20.7% of the time. It means that the interval procedure remains appropriately broad with so little data.

04 /

What counts as an observation?

Before choosing a bound, define the denominator. A missing check-in is not automatically a failure to appear. The person may have attended without being recorded, cancelled before the commitment, or encountered a measurement problem. Those situations should not be silently folded into the same binary outcome.

An illustrative evidence summary might say: “10 show-ups across 10 recorded eligible events; 2 additional events have unknown outcomes and are excluded.” That makes the calculation inspectable. It also reveals a limit: excluding unknowns does not remove bias if missing observations are systematically related to whether someone appeared.

The context belongs beside the count. Which types of event were included? What qualified as a commitment? How was attendance verified, and over what observation window? A summary across very different commitments can look tidy while answering no coherent question. Clear definitions and a route to correct erroneous records are more valuable than extra decimal places.

05 /

Where the model can mislead

The binomial interpretation assumes comparable trials with a common probability and independent outcomes. A real show-up history does not guarantee either condition. Several events may share a transport disruption, a host, or the same recording error. Circumstances and behavior can change over time. A larger n is not a cure for a mismatched model.

The 95% confidence convention describes the long-run coverage of an interval procedure under its assumptions, with coverage approximate because outcomes are discrete. It does not mean there is a 95% probability that this particular person’s future behavior lies in the displayed interval. Nor does the lower endpoint guarantee the next outcome.

There is also a product trade-off: a cautious bound favors established records over new ones, even when both have only successes. That may be useful for expressing evidence strength, but harmful if used as a blanket access gate. Keep low evidence distinct from negative evidence, and examine how the surrounding decision treats newcomers.

06 /

Show the evidence, not a label

Instead of a badge that says “trusted,” show a limited claim: the recorded outcomes, their context, and an uncertainty summary. Let someone understand why ten successes support a stronger signal than one, without implying that either record captures character, intent or every future situation.

A Wilson bound is most useful when it restrains the interface’s confidence. It cannot repair poor observations, unfair consequences or an ambiguous definition of attendance. The point is not to make a human judgment look scientific. It is to say, precisely, how much evidence the calculation has, and how much remains unknown.

Take this with you

The denominator is part of the story.

  • Show counts and the chosen confidence convention, not just a percentage.
  • Separate unknown observations from recorded failures; expose the evidence context.
  • Treat the bound as uncertainty about a defined behavior, never a verdict on a person.
