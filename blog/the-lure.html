Authorized security awareness

The lure is the product

A phishing-awareness programme has one job: to find who is exposed before a real attacker does, and to teach them without shame. Everything in Antercept turns on the quality of a single artefact — the lure — because a test is only as instructive as the message a careful person might, for one honest moment, have believed.

The uncomfortable truth of this field is that a good awareness lure and a real criminal lure are built from the same parts. The difference is not the craft; it is the authorization, the fictional brand, the reserved domain, and the fact that the only thing waiting behind the link is a lesson. Antercept leans into that symmetry on purpose. To teach someone to recognise manipulation, you have to be able to produce it convincingly, label every mechanism inside it, and then dismantle it in front of them.

ⓘ
About the examples

Every brand, sender, domain and person in this chapter is fictional. Antercept composes awareness lures from invented brands on reserved .example names, for authorized testing only. Nothing here is sendable, and no real service is imitated.

You cannot teach someone to see a trap you are unwilling to build in front of them.

Part 1 · The psychology

The six levers of persuasion

Decades of social-engineering research reduce to a short, stubborn list. Almost every deceptive message pulls on one or more of six levers: authority (it appears to come from someone who may instruct you), urgency (it gives you less time than you need to think), familiarity (it wears a brand or a colleague you already trust), curiosity (it dangles something unnamed), fear (it threatens your money, access or standing), and scarcity (it implies a closing window). Antercept treats these as the primitives of composition. You do not write a lure; you choose which levers to pull, and the system assembles language around them.

The reason to make the levers explicit is pedagogical. When a target later sees the debrief, Antercept does not merely say “this was a phishing test.” It highlights the exact clause that carried each lever and names it: this sentence was authority, this deadline was urgency. People do not generalise from “be careful online”; they generalise from seeing the specific hook that nearly caught them.

Part 2 · The AI

How the AI composes a lure

Antercept’s template studio is a pipeline, not a prompt. A brand profile and a channel go in; a structured, annotated lure comes out. The steps are deliberately separable so that each one can be inspected, constrained, and overridden — a black box that simply emitted convincing text would be the wrong tool for a programme whose purpose is to reveal mechanism.

Stage one reads a brand profile: a fictional sender’s kind (webmail, finance, delivery, HR) and tone (clerical, urgent, friendly). Stage two performs lever selection, choosing which of the six hooks to use and in what order — a finance pretext leans on fear and authority, a delivery pretext on curiosity and urgency. Stage three is clause assembly, where each selected lever is rendered into a concrete sentence. Stage four annotates every generated span with the lever it carries, so nothing in the output is unexplained. The result is a lure and, attached to it, a forecast.

The studio does not emit a message; it emits a message with its own X-ray.

Clause assembly, worked

Consider a fictional “Contoso 365” password-expiry pretext on email, with the levers urgency and authority selected. Antercept composes a subject that encodes urgency (“Action required today”), an opening that encodes authority (“an official notice from the Contoso 365 security team”), and a deadline that re-applies urgency (“confirm within the next 30 minutes”). Each span is tagged. The call to action is rendered to the channel: a button in email, a short link in SMS.

Because the composition is structured rather than free-form, it is also constrainable. A programme can forbid certain levers for a vulnerable audience, require the fictional brand to never resemble a real one, or cap difficulty. The same structure that makes the lure convincing makes it governable.

Change the brand, toggle the levers, and the lure re-composes — with every persuasion span dotted and explained on hover. This is the teaching surface: not a message, but a message you can take apart.

Difficulty is a dial, not an accident

Not every target should receive the same test. A new hire and a seasoned finance controller need different pressure to learn something. Antercept models difficulty as the count and intensity of levers, the plausibility of the pretext for the audience, and the subtlety of the tells it leaves in. A level-one lure is almost polite; a level-three lure stacks authority, fear and urgency and hides its seams.

The dial matters because an awareness programme is a curriculum, not a single exam. You start people where they can succeed, raise difficulty as they improve, and reserve the hardest lures for audiences whose compromise would hurt most. Difficulty is how a programme turns one lure into a learning path.

Email and SMS are different animals

A lure is shaped by its channel. Email affords a sender name, a subject, formatting, and a visible link — room for authority and detail. SMS strips all of that away: no subject, little formatting, a bare link, and a cultural expectation of brevity that makes urgency feel natural rather than suspicious. Antercept composes per-channel, not once-then-truncated, because the persuasion that works in an inbox fails on a lock screen and vice versa.

The SMS case is where honesty matters most. A short link on a phone is nearly impossible to vet, which is exactly why it is effective and exactly why the debrief has to teach the one durable rule: a message you did not initiate should never be the thing that takes you to a login.

Part 3 · The forecast

Predicting the click, transparently

Before a campaign is launched, Antercept estimates two numbers: the share of targets who will click, and the share who will report. A black-box score would be useless to a security lead who has to defend the exercise to leadership, so the model is deliberately small and legible — a handful of named factors, each with a visible weight, combined through a logistic function into a probability.

The weights are not fitted on real victims; they are explicit modelling choices that encode a defensible belief: urgency and a well-fitted pretext raise the click rate, a susceptible audience raises it further, and recent training lowers it. Because every term is visible, a security lead can argue with the model — raise the training weight for a department that just completed a course, or lower pretext fit for an audience the lure does not suit — and watch the forecast move.

The number that actually matters

Click rate measures exposure; report rate measures resilience. A mature programme watches the report rate climb more than it watches the click rate fall, because reporting is the behaviour you actually want — a person who clicks and reports has still done the organisation a service. Antercept models report rate as rising with training and falling as the lure becomes more convincing, and bounds it so it can never exceed what the click rate leaves room for.

This is why the forecast reports both. A campaign that is predicted to produce a low click rate and a high report rate is a healthy exercise; one predicted to produce a high click rate and a low report rate is a warning that the audience needs teaching before, not after, the test. The prediction is not there to score people. It is there to decide whether the test is worth running yet.

Product direction · Proposed AI capability

A drafting assistant with a review trail

The next step for the template studio is an AI assistant that turns an approved brief into a draft an operator can explain, edit and approve.

The brief would contain the learning objective, channel, approved brand vocabulary and audience context. The assistant would suggest wording and annotate the cues it introduces: urgency, familiarity or authority. Each suggestion would remain attached to its source brief and version, so the reviewer can see what changed and why.

I would keep the generated draft beside its review state, rather than making generation the final action. Changes to the audience or message would return the draft to review. Approval would preserve the exact version used for the exercise, allowing the final results to be compared with the original assumptions.

Forecasts that admit what they do not know

A proposed outcome model would estimate cohort-level click and report rates from comparable, consented campaign history. The interface would show expected counts, an uncertainty interval and the campaigns behind the comparison. With too little comparable history, it would show “insufficient evidence” and offer a clearly labelled planning scenario instead.

Evaluation would compare forecasts with held-out campaigns over time, checking calibration separately by channel and audience. The composer and equations in this article are illustrative prototypes; no trained model, measured accuracy or production integration is claimed.

Conclusion: the craft is the curriculum

Antercept’s first capability is not “generate phishing.” It is to make the craft of persuasion inspectable: to choose levers deliberately, assemble them into a convincing but fully annotated message, tune difficulty to an audience, and predict the outcome transparently enough to decide whether the lesson is ready to be taught. The lure is the product because the lure, dismantled, is the lesson.

The next chapter follows what happens when the lesson lands — when a target clicks, and a controlled test turns a moment of inattention into clear, measurable evidence of exposure.
